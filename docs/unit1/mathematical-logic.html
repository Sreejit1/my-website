Mathematical Logic

1. Propositions

A proposition is a declarative statement that is either True (T) or False (F), but not both.

Examples

Statement Proposition?
2 + 3 = 5 Yes
Delhi is the capital of India. Yes
10 > 20 Yes
What is your name? No
Close the door. No

Important Point

A proposition must have a definite truth value.

2. Logical Connectives

The basic logical connectives are:

Symbol Name
¬p NOT
p ∧ q AND
p ∨ q OR
p → q Implication
p ↔︎ q Biconditional

3. NOT

The negation of p is written as:

\[ \neg p \]

Truth table:

p ¬p
T F
F T

4. AND

The conjunction of p and q is:

\[ p \land q \]

It is True only when both p and q are True.

p q p ∧ q
T T T
T F F
F T F
F F F

Remember

AND → Both must be True.

5. OR

The disjunction of p and q is:

\[ p \lor q \]

It is False only when both propositions are False.

p q p ∨ q
T T T
T F T
F T T
F F F

Remember

OR → At least one must be True.

6. Implication

Implication is written as:

\[ p \rightarrow q \]

Read as:

If p, then q.

Truth table:

p q p → q
T T T
T F F
F T T
F F T

⭐ UGC NET Important

An implication is False only when:

\[ p=T,\quad q=F \]

Remember:

T → F = F

All other cases are True.

7. Biconditional

Biconditional is written as:

\[ p \leftrightarrow q \]

It is True when both propositions have the same truth value.

p q p ↔︎ q
T T T
T F F
F T F
F F T

8. Tautology

A proposition that is always True is called a tautology.

Example:

\[ p \lor \neg p \]

Truth table:

p ¬p p ∨ ¬p
T F T
F T T

Therefore:

\[ p \lor \neg p \]

is a tautology.

9. Contradiction

A proposition that is always False is called a contradiction.

Example:

\[ p \land \neg p \]

p ¬p p ∧ ¬p
T F F
F T F

Therefore:

\[ p \land \neg p \]

is a contradiction.

10. Contingency

A proposition that is sometimes True and sometimes False is called a contingency.

Example:

\[ p \land q \]

Its truth value depends on the values of p and q.

11. Important Logical Equivalences

Some important equivalences for UGC NET are:

Double Negation

\[ \neg(\neg p) \equiv p \]

De Morgan’s Laws

\[ \neg(p \land q) \equiv \neg p \lor \neg q \]

\[ \neg(p \lor q) \equiv \neg p \land \neg q \]

Implication

\[ p \rightarrow q \equiv \neg p \lor q \]

Biconditional

\[ p \leftrightarrow q \equiv (p \rightarrow q) \land (q \rightarrow p) \]

12. Converse, Inverse and Contrapositive

For:

\[ p \rightarrow q \]

Converse

\[ q \rightarrow p \]

Inverse

\[ \neg p \rightarrow \neg q \]

Contrapositive

\[ \neg q \rightarrow \neg p \]

⭐ Important

The original statement is logically equivalent to its contrapositive:

\[ p \rightarrow q \equiv \neg q \rightarrow \neg p \]

But the converse is not necessarily equivalent to the original statement.

13. Normal Forms

Two important normal forms are:

Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF)

CNF is an AND of OR terms.

Example:

\[ (p \lor q) \land (r \lor s) \]

Structure:

AND of ORs

Disjunctive Normal Form (DNF)

DNF is an OR of AND terms.

Example:

\[ (p \land q) \lor (r \land s) \]

Structure:

OR of ANDs

14. Quick Memory Trick

CNF

C = Conjunction

So:

CNF → AND of ORs

DNF

D = Disjunction

So:

DNF → OR of ANDs

15. Practice Question

Question 1

Which of the following is a tautology?

A) \(p \land \neg p\)

B) \(p \lor \neg p\)

C) \(p \land q\)

D) \(p \rightarrow \neg p\)

Answer

B) \(p \lor \neg p\)

Because it is always True.

16. Practice Question

Question 2

The implication:

\[ p \rightarrow q \]

is false when:

A) p = T, q = T

B) p = T, q = F

C) p = F, q = T

D) p = F, q = F

Answer

B) p = T, q = F
